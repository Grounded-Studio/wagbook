Saying goodbye to your pet: grief, memories and what needs arranging

Published on 12 November 2026 · 5 min read · Lees in het Nederlands

Losing a pet hurts. About grief, talking to children, the practical things to arrange and ways to keep the memory alive.

A pet is family. They were there every day: when you got up, when you came home, on the sofa. When they die, the house is quiet. That grief is real, and it’s allowed. This article isn’t a guide to grieving, because everyone grieves differently. But you’ll find some recognition here, some practical matters and a few ideas for keeping the memory alive.

Grief is allowed

Some people feel almost guilty about how much it hurts ‘over an animal’. But the bond with a pet is often very pure and part of every day. Take your time. Talk about it with people who knew your pet too. And if you still feel very low after a long time, talk to someone about it, for example your doctor.

Talking to children

  • Be honest and use clear words. Say ‘has died’ rather than ‘has gone to sleep’, which can make a child afraid of going to sleep.
  • Let them see that you’re sad too. That way a child learns that grief is normal.
  • Let children take part in saying goodbye if they want to, for example with a drawing, a letter or a spot in the garden.

Other pets in the house

Other pets can notice that their companion is gone. They may search, eat a little less or be more clingy. Keep their routine as normal as possible and give them some extra attention. If it doesn’t get better, or a pet stops eating altogether, ask your vet for advice.

What needs arranging?

  • The body. Your vet can arrange a cremation, individual or shared, and you can often get the ashes back. Burying a small pet in your own garden is often allowed, but the rules differ by place. Check your local rules.
  • The microchip registration. Report the death to the database where your pet’s microchip is registered.
  • Insurance and subscriptions. Cancel pet insurance, food subscriptions or a dog licence.
  • Reminders and appointments. Stop reminders for vaccinations and medication, and cancel planned appointments. That way you won’t be reminded unexpectedly.
💡

You don’t have to do everything at once. Ask someone to help with the practical things, so you have time to say goodbye.

Keeping the memory

  • Pick your favourite photos and make an album or collage.
  • Write down what you don’t want to forget: funny habits, the first day, the last lovely moment.
  • Keep the collar, the tag or a paw print in a memory box.
  • Plant something in the garden or put a plant on their favourite spot.

A new pet?

There’s no right or wrong moment. Some people want another pet to care for soon, others need years. A new pet doesn’t replace the old one. It gets a place of its own, next to the memory.
